<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> tires wear out either by tread or by age, whichever comes first. By tread, many all-season tires last somewhere around 40,000 to 80,000 miles, depending on the tire, the car and how you drive. By age, many vehicle manufacturers recommend replacing tires at about six years old even if the tread looks fine. Check your owner's manual and the tire maker's guidance for your exact tires.</p>

Two tires that look the same can have very different lives. A set on a light commuter car that's rotated and kept at the right pressure can far outlast a set on a heavy car that's driven hard and rarely checked.

[[cta]]

## Tire life in miles: what makes the difference

Each tire model has its own design life, and many come with a treadwear warranty that gives you a rough idea of what the maker expects. Within that, these things decide how long your tires actually last:

- **Pressure.** Underinflated tires wear their edges and run hotter; overinflated ones wear the center. Checking monthly makes a real difference. See [how often to check tire pressure](/guides/how-often-to-check-tire-pressure/).
- **Rotation.** Front and rear tires wear differently. Regular [tire rotation](/guides/how-often-to-rotate-tires/) evens out the wear so the set lasts longer.
- **Alignment.** A car out of alignment can wear one edge of a tire quickly. Uneven wear is often the first sign.
- **Driving style.** Hard acceleration, fast cornering and heavy braking all scrub away tread.
- **Roads and climate.** Rough roads, gravel and high heat all shorten tire life.
- **Tire type.** Performance tires usually trade tread life for grip. Winter tires wear faster when used in warm weather.

## Tire life in years: why age matters

Rubber ages even when a tire isn't being driven. Over time it hardens and can crack, which reduces grip and raises the risk of a failure. That's why a tire with plenty of tread can still be too old.

Many vehicle manufacturers recommend replacing tires around six years old regardless of tread, and some tire makers give an upper limit of ten years. Your owner's manual and the tire maker's website say which applies to you.

To find a tire's age, look for the DOT code on the sidewall. The last four digits are the week and year it was made: "2419" means the 24th week of 2019. Check this when you buy tires too, since a "new" tire may have been in storage for a while.

Age matters most for cars that don't move much, and for spares. Our guide to [low-mileage car maintenance](/guides/low-mileage-car-maintenance/) covers tires that wear out by the calendar long before the odometer.

## How to tell your tires are worn out

- **Tread depth.** The legal minimum in most US states is 2/32 of an inch; check your state's rule. The penny test is a quick check: put a penny into the tread with Lincoln's head upside down and facing you. If you can see the top of his head, the tread is worn to about 2/32 inch or less and the tire should be replaced. Many tire experts suggest replacing tires earlier, around 4/32 inch, if you drive in rain or snow, because grip on wet roads drops as tread wears.
- **Tread wear bars.** Small raised bars across the tread grooves. When the tread is level with them (at 2/32 inch), the tire is worn out.
- **Cracks in the sidewall or tread**, which point to age or damage.
- **Bulges or blisters** on the sidewall. Replace the tire; don't keep driving on it.
- **Uneven wear.** One edge worn more than the other, or patchy wear, usually points to alignment, suspension or pressure problems. Fix the cause or the new tires will wear the same way.
- **New vibration** that wasn't there before.

## Storing seasonal tires

If you swap between summer and winter tires, how you store the off-season set affects how long it lasts:

- **Clean them** and let them dry before storing.
- **Keep them cool, dry and out of direct sunlight**, away from heaters, electric motors and anything that produces ozone, which ages rubber.
- **Mounted tires can be stacked flat or hung; unmounted tires are usually stored upright.** Check the tire maker's guidance.
- **Mark each tire's position** on the car, so you can keep rotating them in the right pattern.

Stored tires still age, so check the DOT date before putting them back on.

## Replacing tires

- **Replace them in pairs** on the same axle, usually, so both sides grip the same.
- **All-wheel-drive cars** often need all four tires closely matched in size and tread depth. Check the owner's manual before replacing only two.
- **Match the size and load rating** on the door-jamb placard or in the manual.
- **Get an alignment check** if the old tires wore unevenly.

Write down the date and mileage of each new set, and the DOT dates of the tires. Then the two questions that matter, how far you've driven on them and how old they are, are always one glance away.
