<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> transmission fluid intervals vary more than almost any other item, from roughly 30,000 to 100,000 miles, and some owner's manuals call it "lifetime" fluid under normal use. If you tow, drive mostly in stop-and-go traffic or live somewhere very hot, your manual's severe schedule probably calls for a change much sooner. The only reliable number is the one in your manual for your exact transmission.</p>

Transmission repairs are among the most expensive a car can need, and the fluid is what keeps that transmission cool and lubricated. Following the interval costs little by comparison.

[[cta]]

## Why intervals vary so much

Transmission fluid cools and lubricates the transmission, and in an automatic it also transmits power and controls shifting. Over time, heat breaks it down and it picks up wear particles. How fast depends on:

- **The transmission type.** Conventional automatics, continuously variable transmissions (CVTs), dual-clutch automatics and manual gearboxes all use different fluids and have different intervals. CVTs and dual-clutch transmissions are especially particular about fluid, so never substitute a general-purpose one.
- **The fluid.** Each transmission specifies its own fluid. Using the wrong type can cause shifting problems or damage.
- **How the car is used.** Heat is the main enemy. Towing, heavy loads, hilly terrain, stop-and-go traffic and hot climates all raise transmission temperatures.

## "Lifetime" fluid

Some manufacturers list no scheduled change under normal driving. That usually means the fluid is expected to last the transmission's design life under ideal conditions.

Many of those same manuals list a change on the severe maintenance schedule, and many drivers' use fits that schedule more often than they think. If you tow, sit in traffic daily or plan to keep the car well past 100,000 miles, ask a dealer or a trusted transmission shop what they recommend for your model.

## Manual transmissions

Manual gearboxes use gear oil or a specific manual transmission fluid, and a few specify automatic transmission fluid or another fluid, so use exactly what the manual lists. Intervals vary by maker; some list a change, some only an inspection. The owner's manual has the details.

## Signs something is wrong

- **Slipping**: engine revs rise but the car doesn't accelerate as it should.
- **Harsh, delayed or erratic shifts.**
- **A delay** before the car moves when you select drive or reverse.
- **A burnt smell.**
- **Red or brown fluid** under the car.
- **A transmission warning light.**

Low fluid from a leak can cause any of these, and so can more serious faults. Have the car checked promptly; driving with a slipping transmission can turn a small repair into a big one.

## Checking the fluid

Some automatics have a dipstick, often with a colored handle; the manual explains how to read it, often with the engine warm and running and the car level, though some say to check in neutral or with the engine off. Many newer transmissions have no dipstick and need a shop to check the level.

Fresh automatic fluid is often red or pink and fairly clear. Dark brown fluid or a burnt smell can mean the fluid is worn or has overheated. Bits or a metallic shine in the fluid can point to internal wear, so have a transmission shop look at it before simply changing the fluid.

## Drain-and-fill vs. flush

A **drain-and-fill** removes the fluid from the pan and refills it. Because much of the fluid stays in the torque converter and lines, this replaces only part of it; some services do it more than once.

A **flush** uses a machine to exchange most of the fluid.

Manufacturers and shops differ on which suits which transmission. Follow your manual, or a shop that works on your model regularly. If a high-mileage transmission has never been serviced, ask for advice before changing the fluid rather than simply ordering the most thorough service.

## Towing and transmission temperature

Heat is what wears transmission fluid out fastest, and towing creates a lot of it. If you tow regularly:

- **Check your manual's towing section** for the severe-service transmission interval and any towing limits.
- **Ask whether your vehicle has a transmission cooler.** Many trucks and SUVs with a towing package have one; others can have one fitted.
- **Use the tow/haul mode** if your vehicle has it, which changes the shift points to reduce heat.
- **Watch the transmission temperature gauge** if the vehicle has one.

Treat towing as the main reason to change the fluid sooner, even if your daily driving is gentle.

## Keeping track

Transmission fluid is easy to lose track of because the interval is long and many cars never get a reminder from the dashboard. When it's done, record the date, the mileage and the fluid used. A record of transmission service is something buyers of high-mileage cars look for; see [keeping service records for resale](/guides/car-service-records-for-resale/).

If your driving fits the severe schedule, use those intervals for the transmission just as you would for [oil changes](/guides/how-often-to-change-oil/).
