<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> a typical car battery lasts about three to five years. Heat, lots of short trips and long periods of sitting push it toward the shorter end, and your owner's manual may give guidance for your car. Once your battery is three years old, have it tested once a year, and replace it when the test or the warning signs say it's weakening, not when it leaves you stranded.</p>

Batteries rarely fail with much notice. One morning the engine cranks a little slower than usual; a few weeks later it doesn't crank at all. Knowing the age of your battery and checking it before it's a problem is the whole trick.

[[cta]]

## What decides how long a battery lasts

A car battery is a chemical device, and the chemistry wears out with time and use. A few things speed that up:

- **Heat.** High under-hood temperatures speed up the chemical breakdown inside the battery. Batteries in hot climates often have shorter lives than the same battery in a mild one.
- **Short trips.** Starting the engine takes a big draw from the battery, and the alternator needs driving time to put that charge back. A car used only for short hops can spend much of its life partly discharged, which wears a battery faster.
- **Sitting.** A parked car still draws a small amount of power for its clock, alarm and computers. Leave it for weeks and the battery slowly drains. See our guide to [maintenance for a low-mileage car](/guides/low-mileage-car-maintenance/) for how to look after one that rarely moves.
- **Electrical load.** Modern cars run more electronics than older ones, and accessories plugged in with the engine off add to the drain.
- **Vibration and loose mounting.** A battery that isn't held down firmly can be damaged internally.

## Signs your battery is getting weak

- **Slow cranking.** The engine turns over more slowly than usual, especially on cold mornings.
- **Clicking when you turn the key or press start**, with little or no cranking.
- **Dim lights or a flickering dashboard** while starting.
- **The battery warning light** on the dashboard. This light usually points to a charging problem, such as the alternator, rather than the battery itself, so have the charging system checked too.
- **A swollen or cracked case, a leak, or a rotten-egg smell.** Don't jump-start or charge it, and keep flames and sparks away. Have it checked or replaced before driving further.
- **White or green corrosion** on the terminals, which can cause poor connections.
- **Age.** Many batteries have a date code or sticker on the case. If yours is past three years, it's worth testing even if it seems fine.

## How to check a battery

The most reliable check is a load test, which measures how the battery performs under a heavy draw like starting the engine. Many auto parts stores in the US test batteries for free, and most shops can do it during other service.

A simple voltmeter reading at rest is a rough first check: a healthy, fully charged 12-volt battery usually reads around 12.6 volts after the car has sat for a while. A noticeably lower reading suggests it's discharged or weak, but a battery can show a reasonable resting voltage and still fail a load test, so treat the voltmeter as a hint, not a verdict.

While you're there, look at the terminals. Corrosion can be cleaned off, and the clamps should be tight.

## When to replace it

Replace the battery when:

- it fails a load test, or the test shows it's close to failing,
- it's old and showing warning signs, or
- you're heading into winter or a long trip with an old battery you don't trust. A [road trip checklist](/guides/road-trip-car-checklist/) is a good moment to decide.

Replace it with the type your owner's manual specifies. Cars with start-stop systems often need an AGM or enhanced flooded battery, and fitting a basic one can shorten its life or cause electrical faults. Some cars also need the new battery registered with the car's computer so the charging system treats it correctly; the manual or a dealer will tell you if yours does.

## Making your next battery last longer

- **Drive longer now and then.** A regular drive long enough to fully warm the engine helps keep the battery charged.
- **Use a battery maintainer** if the car will sit for more than a couple of weeks.
- **Keep the terminals clean and tight.**
- **Don't run accessories for long with the engine off.**
- **Note the install date.** Write down the date and mileage when you fit a new battery, so you know when it's due for testing.

A battery doesn't have a mileage interval in most owner's manuals, which is exactly why it's easy to forget. A yearly reminder to have it tested, starting when the battery is three years old, catches most failures before they happen.
