<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> a car that's barely driven still needs regular service, because oil, brake fluid, coolant, batteries and tires all age with time, not just miles. Follow the <em>time</em> limits in your owner's manual (for oil, commonly 6 or 12 months), even if the car is nowhere near the mileage limit. Use date-based reminders rather than mileage ones, check the battery and tire pressure monthly, and drive the car long enough to fully warm up when you do.</p>

Low-mileage cars sound easy to look after. In practice they're the ones that get neglected, because the usual cue, the odometer approaching a number on a sticker, never arrives.

[[cta]]

## Why low mileage doesn't mean low maintenance

Most service intervals have two limits: miles and time, whichever comes first. A high-mileage driver hits the mileage limit first and never thinks about time. A low-mileage driver hits the time limit first, and if they only track miles, they miss it.

Things that age with time:

- **Engine oil** picks up moisture and combustion byproducts, and its additives break down. Short trips make this worse, because the engine never gets hot enough to burn off moisture.
- **Brake fluid** absorbs moisture from the air over time, which is why many manuals give it a time-based interval rather than a mileage one.
- **Coolant** has a time limit as well as a mileage one in most manuals.
- **Batteries** discharge slowly when the car sits, and a battery that's repeatedly run low wears out sooner.
- **Tires** lose air and age whether or not they're driven.
- **Wiper blades** and other rubber parts harden with age and sun.

## Is your car on the severe schedule?

Many low-mileage cars are driven mostly on short trips: to the store, to school, around town. Owner's manuals typically list short trips, especially in cold weather, as a severe operating condition. That can mean a shorter oil interval, not a longer one.

Read the severe-schedule description in your manual. If most of your drives are a few miles, it probably applies. See [how often to change your oil](/guides/how-often-to-change-oil/) for more on the two schedules.

## A maintenance plan for a car that barely moves

### Set reminders by date

Mileage reminders rarely fire on a car like this, because it may never reach them. Date-based reminders are the ones that will actually remind you. Set them for:

- **Oil and filter:** at the time limit in your manual, commonly every 6 or 12 months
- **Brake fluid:** at the manual's time interval, if it gives one
- **Coolant:** at the time limit in the manual
- **Wiper blades:** once a year or so
- **Battery test:** yearly once the battery is a few years old
- **Registration and inspection:** by their due dates

You can keep the mileage interval as a backstop in case the car's use changes, but make sure the time limit is there for each item. An [oil change reminder app](/guides/oil-change-reminder-app/) that tracks both and alerts at whichever comes first handles this automatically.

### Check monthly

- **Tire pressure,** cold, set to the door-jamb placard. A car that sits loses pressure like any other.
- **The battery,** if the car is slow to start or sits for weeks.
- **Under the car,** for fresh drips or leaks on the ground where it's parked.
- **Signs of animals,** especially if the car is parked outside or in a barn. Rodents nest in engine bays and chew wiring.

### Drive it properly

When you do drive the car, go far enough that the engine reaches full operating temperature: a drive of 20 minutes or so on a mix of roads is better than several two-mile trips. That burns off moisture in the oil and exhaust, charges the battery, and keeps the brakes from developing surface rust.

### Look after the battery

If the car regularly sits for more than a couple of weeks, a battery maintainer (a smart charger that keeps the battery topped up) is inexpensive insurance. Some modern cars draw a small current even when off, which can drain a battery in weeks.

### Watch tire age, not just tread

Low-mileage cars often have tires with plenty of tread that are simply old. Rubber hardens and cracks with age, so on a car that barely moves, the tires usually reach their age limit long before their tread limit. Check the manufacture date on the sidewall and your owner's manual's guidance; [how long do tires last](/guides/how-long-do-tires-last/) explains how to read the date code and what age limits manufacturers give.

### Keep the fuel fresh

Gasoline deteriorates over months. If the car sits for long stretches, keep the tank reasonably full and use a fuel stabilizer if it will sit for more than a month or two. Driving it regularly enough to burn through a tank every few months avoids the issue.

## Keep logging even with few miles

Low-mileage cars are attractive to buyers, but a buyer will want to see that the car was serviced on time even though it wasn't driven much. Log each service with the date and odometer reading, including oil changes done at the time limit with only a few hundred miles on the oil. That record is the difference between "low miles" and "low miles, well kept."
