<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> brake pads commonly last anywhere from about 25,000 to 70,000 miles, and some last longer. How and where you drive matters a lot: stop-and-go city driving, heavy loads and hills wear pads far faster than steady highway miles. Most owner's manuals don't give a replacement mileage; they say to inspect the brakes regularly and replace the pads when they're worn.</p>

That makes brake pads different from oil or filters. There's no number to count down to, so the only way to know is to look, or to notice the warning signs.

[[cta]]

## What wears brake pads faster

Every time you brake, a little of the pad material wears away. Anything that means more or harder braking shortens pad life:

- **City driving.** Frequent stops at lights and in traffic use the brakes far more than highway driving.
- **Driving style.** Late, hard braking wears pads faster than easing off early.
- **Weight.** Heavy vehicles, full loads and towing all ask more of the brakes.
- **Hills.** Long descents and steep streets add a lot of braking.
- **Pad material.** Organic, semi-metallic and ceramic pads wear at different rates and suit different cars. Replacement pads should match what the car's maker specifies or recommends.

The front pads usually do most of the work, so they tend to wear out before the rears.

## Signs your brake pads need replacing

- **Squealing when you brake.** Many pads have a small metal tab that squeals against the rotor when the pad gets thin. It's designed to be annoying so you'll book a check.
- **Grinding or scraping.** This can mean the pad material is gone and metal is touching metal. Avoid driving the car until it has been inspected: braking may be much weaker than normal, and it can badly damage the rotors. If you must move it, drive slowly and gently to a shop, or have it towed.
- **A brake warning light.** Some cars have pad wear sensors that turn on a light. Others light up for low brake fluid or a parking brake that's still applied. A red brake light that stays on with the parking brake released can mean a serious brake problem, so check your manual and have it inspected promptly.
- **Longer stopping distances, or a brake pedal that feels soft, spongy or sinks toward the floor.** A soft or sinking pedal can mean a fluid leak or another hydraulic fault. Don't keep driving; have the car inspected or towed.
- **The car pulling to one side** when you brake.
- **Visibly thin pads.** On many cars you can see the outer pad through the wheel spokes.

## How thin is too thin?

New pads typically have around 10 millimeters or more of friction material. Many shops recommend replacing them when they're down to about 3 millimeters, and some suggest doing it a little sooner. Your mechanic can measure them, and some manufacturers publish a minimum thickness for their pads. When in doubt, replace them; worn pads cost more if they damage the rotors.

## When to have your brakes inspected

Since there's no fixed interval, tie the inspection to something you already do. A [tire rotation](/guides/how-often-to-rotate-tires/) is the natural moment: the wheels are already off and the pads, rotors and calipers are easy to see. Many shops check brakes at every rotation or oil change; ask them to note the pad thickness on the invoice.

Write that number down. If the front pads measure 6 millimeters at one service and 4 at the next, you have a good idea when they'll be due.

## Hybrids, EVs and cars that sit a lot

Hybrids and electric cars use regenerative braking, where the motor slows the car and recovers energy. The friction brakes do less work, so pads often last much longer. That doesn't mean the brakes can be ignored: brakes that are rarely used can develop rust on the rotors or sticking caliper parts. Follow the inspection interval in the manual even if the pads look thick.

The same applies to cars that sit for long periods.

## Driving habits that make pads last longer

How you drive changes pad life a great deal:

- **Look ahead and ease off early.** Coasting toward a red light instead of braking late takes a lot of wear off the pads.
- **Use lower gears on long descents** if your manual recommends it, so the brakes aren't doing all the work.
- **Don't ride the brakes**, resting your foot on the pedal.
- **Carry less weight.** A trunk full of things you don't need makes every stop a little harder.
- **Leave more space** in traffic, so you can slow gradually instead of stopping hard.

These habits also keep the brakes cooler, which is kinder to the rotors.

## Replacing pads

- **Replace pads per axle**, both sides at once.
- **Have the rotors checked at the same time.** They may be fine, need resurfacing, or need replacing.
- **Use pads that suit your car** and how you drive.
- **New pads need bedding in.** Follow the shop's or the pad maker's instructions for the first miles.

Keep the invoice and note the mileage. Brake work is one of the things a used-car buyer asks about, and a record of when the pads were done answers it.
