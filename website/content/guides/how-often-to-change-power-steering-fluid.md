<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> many owner's manuals list no fixed interval for changing power steering fluid, only regular checks of the level, and many newer cars have electric power steering with no fluid at all. If your car has hydraulic power steering, check the level when you check the other fluids, use only the type your owner's manual specifies, and follow any change interval it gives.</p>

Power steering fluid is one of the quieter items on a car. It rarely needs attention, which is why it's easy to forget until the steering starts to groan.

[[cta]]

## Hydraulic or electric?

There are two kinds of power steering:

- **Hydraulic power steering** uses a pump, usually driven by the engine's serpentine belt, to push fluid that helps turn the wheels. It has a fluid reservoir in the engine bay.
- **Electric power steering** uses an electric motor instead. There's no pump and no fluid to check or change.

Many newer cars use electric steering. Your owner's manual will tell you which you have: if it describes a power steering fluid reservoir and how to check it, you have hydraulic steering. Some systems are electro-hydraulic, using an electric pump with fluid, and they also need fluid checks.

## How often to change it

For hydraulic systems, intervals vary by manufacturer:

- **Many manuals list no change interval**, only inspection of the level and the hoses.
- **Some give a mileage or time interval.**
- **Some mechanics suggest a change** when the fluid is dark, smells burnt or is contaminated, even without a scheduled interval.

Follow your manual first. If it gives no interval and the fluid looks clean, there's usually no reason to change it.

## How to check the level

1. **Find the reservoir.** It's usually a small plastic tank near the belt end of the engine, often with a steering-wheel symbol on the cap. The manual shows where.
2. **Check whether the manual says hot or cold.** Many reservoirs have separate HOT and COLD marks, and some caps have a small dipstick.
3. **Read the level** against the right mark.
4. **Top up only with the specified fluid.** Some cars use a dedicated power steering fluid, some older ones use automatic transmission fluid (only if the manual specifies it), and some need a specific manufacturer fluid. The wrong type can damage seals.

## Signs of a power steering problem

- **Whining or groaning** when turning the wheel, especially at low speeds or at full lock.
- **Heavier steering** than usual.
- **Jerky or uneven assistance.**
- **Fluid under the front of the car**, or wet, oily hoses.
- **A squealing belt** when turning, which can mean the belt is slipping as the pump works harder.

Since the pump is belt-driven on most hydraulic systems, a worn or slipping serpentine belt can also affect steering; see [serpentine and timing belt replacement](/guides/serpentine-and-timing-belt-replacement/).

## Steering problems that aren't the fluid

Heavy or odd-feeling steering isn't always a power steering fault. Before assuming the fluid or pump is to blame, consider:

- **Low tire pressure**, which makes steering noticeably heavier, especially at low speeds.
- **Wheel alignment**, which can make the car pull to one side or the steering wheel sit off center.
- **Worn suspension or steering parts**, such as tie rod ends or ball joints, which can cause looseness, clunks or wandering.
- **On electric power steering**, a warning light and suddenly heavy steering often point to an electrical fault in the system, which needs a shop's diagnostic tools.

A quick tire pressure check is the cheapest first step.

## Leaks

Power steering fluid shouldn't need regular topping up. If the level keeps dropping, there's a leak, often from a hose, a fitting, the pump or the steering rack. Small leaks tend to grow, and running the pump low on fluid can damage it.

## Refreshing old fluid

If your manual gives a change interval, a shop will usually drain or exchange the fluid according to the manufacturer's procedure. On systems without one, some owners and shops refresh dark fluid gradually: removing fluid from the reservoir, refilling with fresh fluid of the right type, and repeating over several drives.

Whichever way it's done, use only the specified fluid, don't overfill past the mark, and check for leaks afterwards. If the steering noise or heaviness that prompted the change doesn't go away, the pump or rack needs a proper check.
