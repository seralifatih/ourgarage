<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> most cars should have their tires rotated every 5,000 to 7,500 miles. Your owner's manual lists the exact interval for your car, and some manufacturers specify a different range. If you don't have a mileage number handy, rotating at every oil change (or every other one, if your oil interval is long) is an easy way to stay close.</p>

Rotation means moving each tire to a different position on the car so they all wear at about the same rate. It's one of the cheapest services there is, and it's the main thing that gets you the full life out of a set of tires.

[[cta]]

## Why tires wear unevenly

Each corner of a car works differently. On a front-wheel-drive car, the front tires steer, put the engine's power to the road and do most of the braking, so they wear faster than the rears. On rear-wheel-drive cars the pattern is different but still uneven. Turning, road crown and how the car is loaded add their own wear patterns on top.

Left alone, one pair of tires reaches the end of its life well before the other. Rotating spreads the wear out so the set wears down together. That gets more miles out of the set, and it means you replace all four at once rather than buying in pairs at awkward times.

There are two more reasons to keep up with it:

- **All-wheel drive.** Many AWD systems are sensitive to differences in tread depth between tires, because a smaller-diameter tire spins at a different speed. Manufacturers of AWD cars generally stress keeping all four tires evenly worn, and regular rotation is how you do that.
- **Tire warranties.** Many tire makers offer a treadwear (mileage) warranty, and many of those require that the tires were rotated at regular intervals. If you ever make a claim, you may be asked for proof. A dated record with the mileage is that proof.

## Finding your interval

Look in the maintenance schedule of your owner's manual. Rotation is usually listed alongside oil changes and inspections, as a mileage. Some manuals tie it directly to the oil change interval; others give a separate number.

If your manual says nothing specific, the 5,000 to 7,500 mile range is the common recommendation from tire makers and shops. Your tire maker's warranty terms may also name an interval. If they differ from the car's manual, follow whichever is shorter so you stay within the warranty.

## Rotation patterns

The pattern depends on the drivetrain and the tires. Your owner's manual usually shows a diagram. The common ones:

| Pattern | Typically used for | How it moves |
|---|---|---|
| Forward cross | Front-wheel drive | Fronts go straight back; rears cross to the opposite front |
| Rearward cross | Rear-wheel drive, many AWD and 4WD | Rears go straight forward; fronts cross to the opposite rear |
| X pattern | Some FWD and AWD cars | All four cross diagonally |
| Front to back | Directional tires | Swap front and rear on the same side only |

Two cases limit your options:

- **Directional tires** are designed to roll one way. The sidewall has an arrow showing the direction. They stay on the same side of the car and only move front to back, unless they're remounted on the wheels.
- **Staggered setups** have different tire sizes front and rear, common on some performance cars. Those tires can't move front to back. Depending on the tires, they may swap side to side or not rotate at all.

If you have a full-size matching spare, some manuals include it in a five-tire rotation. Temporary "donut" spares are never part of the rotation.

## What a good shop does at the same visit

A rotation takes the wheels off, which is a good chance to look at things you otherwise can't see easily. Many shops will, or you can ask them to:

- Check tread depth on each tire and note any uneven wear
- Look at the brakes while the wheels are off
- Set the tire pressures to the values on the placard in your driver's door jamb
- Torque the lug nuts to the car's specification

Uneven wear that rotation doesn't fix, such as one edge of a tire wearing much faster than the other, often points to an alignment problem or worn suspension parts. Rotation hides that pattern by moving the tire, so it's worth asking the shop to point it out before they rotate.

## After the rotation

Check two things in your owner's manual:

1. **Tire pressure monitoring.** Some cars need the system reset or the sensors relearned when tires change position, especially if the car shows each tire's pressure by location. Others do it automatically.
2. **Front and rear pressures.** If your door-jamb placard lists different pressures for front and rear, the pressures need adjusting after the tires move. Our guide on [how often to check tire pressure](/guides/how-often-to-check-tire-pressure/) explains how to do it accurately.

## How to remember it

Rotation is easy to forget because nothing tells you it's due. There's no warning light, and the uneven wear builds up slowly enough that you won't notice it from the driver's seat.

What works for most people:

- **Pair it with your oil change.** If your [oil change interval](/guides/how-often-to-change-oil/) is 5,000 to 7,500 miles, ask for a rotation every time. If it's longer, rotate at the halfway point or at every oil change, depending on your manual.
- **Set a mileage reminder.** Note the odometer reading at the rotation and set a reminder for the next one. Add a time limit too, such as every 6 months, so a low-mileage car still gets looked at.
- **Write down the date and mileage.** That record is what a tire warranty claim asks for, and it's what a buyer looks for when you sell.
