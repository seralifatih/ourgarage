<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> check your tire pressure at least once a month and before any long trip, when the tires are cold. Inflate to the pressure on the placard in your driver's door jamb (also in your owner's manual), not the number on the tire sidewall. This is NHTSA's recommendation, and it takes about five minutes.</p>

Tires lose air slowly, even when nothing is wrong with them, and cold weather lowers the pressure further. You usually can't tell by looking: a radial tire can be well below its correct pressure and still look normal.

[[cta]]

## Why it matters

Pressure is the one tire setting you control, and it affects almost everything the tire does:

- **Safety.** Underinflated tires flex more and build up heat, which raises the risk of a failure. They also make braking and handling less predictable.
- **Tire life.** Underinflation wears the outer edges faster; overinflation wears the center. Either way the tires wear out early, and [rotating them](/guides/how-often-to-rotate-tires/) can't fully even it out.
- **Fuel economy.** Low tires roll with more resistance, so the engine works harder.

## Finding the right pressure

Your car's recommended pressure is printed on a placard, usually on the driver's door jamb. Some cars put it inside the fuel door or the glovebox. The owner's manual lists it too.

A few details people miss:

- **Front and rear can differ.** Many placards list separate pressures for the front and rear tires.
- **Loaded vs. normal.** Some placards or manuals give a higher pressure for a fully loaded car or for sustained highway driving. If you're packing for a trip, check.
- **The sidewall number is a maximum.** It tells you the most the tire can safely hold. Inflating to it usually overinflates the tire for your car.
- **Different tire size?** If the tires on your car aren't the size on the placard, ask a tire shop for the right pressure for those tires.

## How to check it accurately

1. **Start cold.** Check before driving, or after the car has been parked for at least a few hours. A short drive to the gas station raises the pressure enough to throw off the reading.
2. **Use your own gauge.** An inexpensive digital or dial gauge is more trustworthy than the one built into a gas station air hose, which gets dropped and driven over.
3. **Check all four, plus the spare.** Remove the valve cap, press the gauge on firmly and read it. A spare that hasn't been checked in years is often flat when you need it.
4. **Add air to the placard number.** If you're filling at a station after driving there, it's fine to add air to a little above the target and then check again once the tires have cooled, or add the difference you measured when cold.
5. **Put the caps back on.** They keep dirt and moisture out of the valve.

## Temperature and seasons

Air contracts when it cools. A common rule of thumb is that tire pressure changes by about 1 psi for every 10°F change in temperature. So tires set correctly on a warm fall afternoon can be several psi low on the first cold morning of winter.

That's why the tire pressure warning light often comes on during the first cold snap of the year. The fix is the same: check all four tires cold and bring them back up to the placard pressure. In seasons when temperatures swing a lot, checking more often than monthly is worth it.

## The tire pressure warning light (TPMS)

Most cars sold in the US since the late 2000s have a tire pressure monitoring system. Federal rules require it to warn the driver when one or more tires are 25% or more below the recommended pressure. On a tire that should be at 35 psi, that's roughly 26 psi before the light comes on.

So the light is an alarm for a significant problem, not a substitute for checking. Some other things to know:

- **Steady light:** at least one tire is low. Check and inflate as soon as you safely can.
- **Flashing light, then steady:** usually a fault in the system itself, such as a sensor with a dead battery. The system may not warn you about low pressure until it's repaired.
- **Light stays on after inflating:** some cars need a drive of a few minutes or a manual reset. The owner's manual explains how.

## If one tire keeps losing air

All tires lose a little pressure over time. If one tire needs air much more often than the others, there's a reason: a nail or screw in the tread, a leaking valve stem, or a poor seal between the tire and the wheel. A tire shop can find and usually repair it. Don't keep topping it up indefinitely; a slow leak can become a fast one.

## Making it a habit

Monthly is easy to say and easy to forget, because nothing about the car reminds you until the warning light comes on, and by then the tire is already well low. The habit that sticks for most people is a fixed trigger:

- **A monthly reminder**, for example on the first weekend of the month
- **A pre-trip check**, as part of a [road trip checklist](/guides/road-trip-car-checklist/)
- **The first cold morning of the season**, when pressures drop the most

If more than one person drives the car, agree on who does it. Otherwise everyone assumes someone else checked.
