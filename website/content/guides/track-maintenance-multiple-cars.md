<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> put every car in one system, but give each car its own intervals, history and odometer reading. Set each car's reminders from its own owner's manual, since a 2012 sedan and a 2022 SUV won't share a schedule. Give each car a named person who makes sure its services happen, stagger services so they don't all land in the same month, and keep the odometer readings current for every car, not just the one you drive.</p>

Tracking one car is mostly a matter of remembering. With two or more, memory stops working: the dates blur together, receipts end up in the wrong glovebox, and the car nobody drives much quietly goes two years without an oil change.

[[cta]]

## One system, separate records

The most common mistake is a different method for each car: one in the shop's app, one on a sticky note, one in someone's head. Pick one system for all of them:

- **Paper:** a binder with a section per car, each with its own log and "what's due next" page. Our [car maintenance log template](/guides/car-maintenance-log-template/) has both; print a copy per car.
- **Spreadsheet:** one tab per car, plus a summary tab showing what's due next across all of them.
- **App:** one app that holds every vehicle, with reminders and history kept separately for each.

Within that system, each car needs its own:

- Intervals, copied from its own owner's manual
- Service history with dates and odometer readings
- Current odometer reading
- Notes: oil type, tire size, quirks ("check the rear left tire, it loses air")

## Set up each car from its own manual

It's tempting to give every car the same "oil every 5,000 miles, rotate every 5,000" schedule. Don't. Intervals vary between manufacturers, engines and model years, and between normal and severe driving. The family's older car may need more frequent oil changes than the newer one, or the reverse.

Do it once, properly, for each car:

1. Find the owner's manual (most manufacturers publish them online by model and year).
2. Decide whether the car is on the normal or severe schedule, based on how it's actually driven. The car that does short school runs in winter may be on severe; the highway commuter may not.
3. Copy the main intervals: oil, tire rotation, filters, brake fluid, coolant, spark plugs, transmission fluid, plus registration and inspection dates.
4. Enter the last-done date and mileage for each, from receipts or the shop's records.

For typical ranges to check against, see the [car maintenance schedule by mileage](/guides/car-maintenance-schedule-by-mileage/).

## Keep every odometer current

This is where multi-car tracking usually breaks. Mileage-based reminders only work if the system knows each car's mileage. You'll naturally update the car you drive. The second car, the weekend car or the one your teenager drives won't get updated unless you make it a routine.

Pick a day each month and update all of them at once. It takes a minute: one number per car. Some apps prompt you to do this.

## Give each car an owner

Shared visibility is good; shared responsibility for everything is how things get missed. For each car, name one person who:

- Watches what's coming due
- Books the service
- Makes sure it gets logged afterward

That's usually the main driver. Anyone else who takes the car in or does a job logs it too. Our guide to [sharing car maintenance with family](/guides/share-car-maintenance-with-family/) covers how to split this up in a household.

## Stagger services

If all your cars come due in the same month, you get one expensive month and a week where nobody has a car. Staggering helps:

- When you next service each car, let them settle into different months. A car serviced in March and one serviced in June will tend to stay apart.
- Book one car at a time, so there's always one available.
- Keep seasonal work together where it makes sense, such as swapping to winter tires on all cars the same weekend.

## Watch the low-mileage car

In a multi-car household, there's often a car that barely moves: a second car, a weekend car, a car kept for a student home from college. It's the one most likely to be neglected, because it never reaches its mileage limit.

Time limits matter most for that car. Oil, brake fluid, coolant, batteries and tires all age even when the car sits. Make sure its reminders include the time interval from the manual, not just the mileage.

## A simple monthly routine

Once a month, ten minutes for all your cars:

1. Update each car's odometer reading
2. Look at what's due in the next month across all cars
3. Book anything that's due, staggering if needed
4. Check that last month's services were logged
5. Do the quick checks (tire pressure, oil level, lights) on any car that hasn't had them
