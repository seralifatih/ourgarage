<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> it depends on the type of spark plug your engine uses. Basic copper plugs are often replaced around 30,000 miles, while the iridium or platinum plugs fitted to most newer cars commonly last 60,000 to 100,000 miles or more. Your owner's manual gives the exact mileage for your engine, sometimes with a time limit too.</p>

Spark plugs are one of the long-interval items that slip past people. The interval is so long that by the time it comes round, nobody remembers when they were last done, or whether they ever were.

[[cta]]

## What spark plugs do

In a gasoline engine, each cylinder has a spark plug that fires a spark to ignite the fuel and air. Over many miles the plug's electrodes wear down and the gap between them widens, so the plug needs more voltage to fire. Eventually that shows up as misfires, rougher running and wasted fuel.

Diesel engines don't use spark plugs; they use glow plugs for cold starts, which are a different part with their own replacement guidance. Electric cars have neither.

## Plug types and their typical lifespans

| Plug type | Typical replacement range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Copper (nickel alloy) | Often around 30,000 miles | Common in older engines and some performance applications |
| Platinum | Often 60,000 to 100,000 miles | Longer-lasting electrodes |
| Iridium | Often 60,000 to 100,000+ miles | Fitted to many modern engines |

These are general ranges. The interval for your engine is in the maintenance schedule of your owner's manual, and it assumes the plug type the manufacturer fitted. If you or a previous owner switched to a different type, the interval changes with it.

Some manuals also list a time limit, and many specify a shorter interval on the severe maintenance schedule.

## Signs your spark plugs are worn

- **Rough idle**, where the engine shakes or runs unevenly at a stop.
- **Misfires**: a stumble, jerk or hesitation when you accelerate.
- **Harder starting**, especially when cold.
- **Lower fuel economy** than you're used to.
- **A check engine light**, often with a misfire code that a shop or a code reader can read.

None of these is unique to spark plugs. Ignition coils, fuel injectors and vacuum leaks can cause the same symptoms. If the plugs aren't near their interval, have the problem diagnosed before replacing parts.

## What old spark plugs can tell you

When plugs come out, their tips can hint at how the engine is running. A mechanic reads them as clues, not a diagnosis:

- **Light tan or gray deposits** usually mean the plug was working normally.
- **Black, dry, sooty deposits** can point to a rich fuel mixture or a lot of short-trip driving.
- **Wet or oily deposits** can mean oil is getting into the cylinder.
- **White, blistered or melted tips** can point to overheating or the wrong heat range.
- **One plug that looks different from the rest** suggests a problem in that cylinder.

Ask the shop to show you the old plugs, or photograph them yourself if you do the job. If one looks unusual, it's worth mentioning at your next service.

## What replacing them involves

On some engines the plugs are easy to reach and replacing them is a quick job. On others, particularly some V6 engines, the rear plugs sit under the intake manifold, which adds labor. Ask for a quote that includes everything.

If you do it yourself:

- **Use the plug your manual specifies**, including the correct heat range.
- **Check the gap** if the plug maker says it needs setting; many modern plugs come pre-gapped.
- **Tighten to the specified torque.** Over-tightening can damage the threads in the cylinder head.
- **Follow the plug maker's guidance on anti-seize.** Some plugs have a coating and shouldn't have anti-seize added.
- **Work on a cold engine.**

Replace all the plugs as a set. It's also a good moment to check the ignition coils or plug wires for cracks or damage.

## Plug or ignition coil?

Many modern engines have one ignition coil per cylinder, sitting directly on top of the spark plug. When a cylinder misfires, the cause can be the plug, the coil, or something else entirely, like a fuel injector.

Shops often narrow it down by swapping the coil from the misfiring cylinder with another one and seeing whether the misfire moves. If it follows the coil, the coil is at fault; if it stays, the plug or something else is. That's why it's worth a diagnosis before buying a full set of coils along with the plugs.

## Keeping track of it

A 60,000- or 100,000-mile interval is easy to lose track of, especially if you bought the car used. Look through the service records for the last spark plug change. If there's no record and the car is near or past the interval, it's worth having them checked or changed.

Once they're done, record the date, the mileage and the plug type.
