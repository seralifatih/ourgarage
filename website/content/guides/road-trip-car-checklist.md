<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> about a week before a road trip, check the tires (pressure, tread and spare), the oil level and when your next oil change is due, the other fluid levels, all lights and the wipers, the battery's age, and how the brakes feel. Doing it a week out leaves time to fix anything you find. The night before, just check tire pressure again and pack an emergency kit.</p>

Most roadside breakdowns come from things a quick check would have caught: a tire that was already low, a battery near the end of its life, a coolant level nobody looked at. None of these checks need tools beyond a tire gauge and a flashlight.

## One week before

### Tires

Tires carry the most risk on a long drive at highway speed, so start here.

- **Pressure.** Check all four with the tires cold and set them to the pressure on the placard in your driver's door jamb. If you'll be carrying a full load of people and luggage, look for a higher "loaded" pressure on the placard or in the owner's manual. Our guide on [how often to check tire pressure](/guides/how-often-to-check-tire-pressure/) covers how to do it accurately.
- **Tread.** The quick check is the penny test: put a penny into the tread groove with Lincoln's head upside down and facing you. If you can see the top of his head, the tread is at or below 2/32 of an inch, which is the legal minimum in most states, and the tires should be replaced. Check a few spots on each tire, including the inner and outer edges.
- **Damage.** Look for bulges, cuts or cracks in the sidewalls, and for nails or screws in the tread.
- **Spare.** Check its pressure (often higher for temporary spares; it's in the manual) and make sure the jack and lug wrench are in the car. Some newer cars have an inflator kit instead of a spare. Know which you have.

### Oil and the next oil change

Check the oil level on the dipstick or the dashboard display, with the car on level ground. Then do the math on your next change: add the trip's round-trip miles to your current odometer reading. If that total passes your next oil change mileage, or the trip runs past the due date, get it done before you leave. Long highway drives are easy on oil, but there's no reason to start one already due. See [how often to change your oil](/guides/how-often-to-change-oil/) if you're not sure what your interval is.

### Other fluids

With the engine cold, check:

- **Coolant** in the overflow reservoir, between the min and max marks. Never open a hot radiator cap.
- **Brake fluid** in its reservoir. If it's low, have the brakes checked; brake fluid doesn't get used up under normal conditions, so a low level can mean worn pads or a leak.
- **Washer fluid.** Fill it. Bugs on a highway windshield go through it quickly.

Your owner's manual shows where each reservoir is.

### Battery

Find out how old the battery is. Many have a date sticker on top. Batteries commonly last around three to five years, and heat shortens their life. If yours is in that range or older, have it tested before the trip. Many auto parts stores in the US test batteries for free. Also look at the terminals for white or green corrosion.

### Lights and wipers

Walk around the car with someone else at the wheel, or use your reflection in a garage door or shop window. Check headlights (low and high beam), turn signals, brake lights, tail lights and hazard lights. Wipers that streak or chatter are due for replacement; you'll want them in a sudden storm.

### Brakes, steering and anything that feels off

On a normal drive, pay attention to the brakes and steering. Squealing or grinding when braking, a pedal that feels soft or sinks, pulling to one side, or vibration through the steering wheel are all worth a mechanic's look before a long drive. So is any warning light on the dashboard.

### Air conditioning and heat

Run both. A weak A/C is merely uncomfortable on a short drive and miserable on a long one in summer, and it can take a few days to get a repair appointment.

## The night before

- Check tire pressure again, cold, and adjust for the load you're carrying.
- Make sure registration and proof of insurance are in the car.
- Save your roadside assistance number in your phone, whether it comes from your insurer, a membership or your car's manufacturer.
- Load the emergency kit (below).

## A basic emergency kit

- Phone charger and a cable that works in your car
- Flashlight with fresh batteries
- First aid kit
- Jumper cables or a portable jump starter
- Tire pressure gauge
- Water and some snacks
- A blanket, and in winter, an ice scraper, gloves and warm clothes
- Reflective triangles or flares

## If you'll be towing or carrying a heavy load

Check your owner's manual for the vehicle's towing and payload limits, and for any tire pressure changes for heavy loads. Towing also usually puts you on the manual's severe maintenance schedule, which means shorter intervals for oil and other fluids after the trip.

## After the trip

A long trip can add a lot of miles quickly. Update your odometer reading when you get home so any mileage-based reminders, such as oil changes and tire rotations, move closer to reality. If you noticed anything odd on the road, write it down while you remember the details.

If several people share the car, tell them what you checked and what's coming due. Our guide to [sharing car maintenance with family](/guides/share-car-maintenance-with-family/) covers how to keep everyone on the same page without a group chat full of "did anyone check the tires?"
