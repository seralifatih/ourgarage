<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> it depends on the coolant your car uses. Older conventional coolants were often changed around every 30,000 miles or two years, while many modern long-life coolants are rated for 100,000 miles or more, or several years. Your owner's manual gives the exact interval, usually as a mileage and a time limit, whichever comes first.</p>

Coolant, often called antifreeze, keeps the engine at the right temperature in summer heat and stops it freezing in winter. It's another long-interval item that's easy to lose track of, and an overheated engine is an expensive way to find out.

[[cta]]

## What coolant does

A liquid-cooled engine pumps coolant through passages around the cylinders, then through the radiator to shed the heat. The coolant:

- carries heat away from the engine,
- keeps the system from freezing in cold weather,
- raises the boiling point so it doesn't boil in hot weather, and
- protects metal and rubber parts from corrosion.

Over time the additives that prevent corrosion wear out. The coolant can look fine while no longer protecting the system, which is why it's changed on a schedule.

## How often to change it

The interval depends on the coolant type the manufacturer fills at the factory.

| Coolant type | Typical first change |
|---|---|
| Older conventional (often called IAT) | Often around 30,000 miles or two years |
| Long-life (OAT, HOAT and similar) | Often 100,000 miles or more, or several years |

These are general patterns, not rules. Many long-life coolants have a longer first interval and shorter ones after that. Your manual has the numbers, and many cars also have a label near the radiator or reservoir.

## Coolant types and colors

Coolants use different chemistries, and they're not all compatible. Color isn't a reliable guide: makers use different dyes, and the same color can mean different chemistry from brand to brand.

- **Use the type your manual specifies**, by its specification or the manufacturer's part name, not by color.
- **Don't mix types.** If you're not sure what's in the car, ask a shop to check, or have the system flushed and refilled with the right one.
- **Mixed or concentrated?** Many coolants are sold premixed at 50/50 with water. Concentrate must be mixed in the right ratio, often 50/50 but not always, usually with distilled water. Undiluted concentrate cools worse than a proper mix. The product label and your manual say how.

## How to check the coolant level safely

1. **Wait until the engine is cold.** Never open a radiator cap or a pressurized reservoir on a hot engine. The coolant is under pressure and can spray out scalding hot.
2. **Find the reservoir**, a translucent plastic tank with MIN and MAX marks. The manual shows where.
3. **Check the level** is between the marks.
4. **Top up with the correct coolant** if it's low.

Checking it monthly is easy, and it's one of the checks on our [road trip checklist](/guides/road-trip-car-checklist/).

## Signs of a coolant problem

- **The temperature gauge running higher** than usual, or a warning light.
- **Steam** from under the hood.
- **A sweet smell**, inside or outside the car.
- **Puddles or drips**, often green, orange, pink or blue, under the front of the car.
- **A level that keeps dropping.**
- **Weak heat** from the heater in winter.

If the engine starts to overheat, pull over safely and turn it off. Driving an overheating engine can cause serious damage. Let the engine cool completely before opening the radiator cap or the reservoir cap, because hot coolant can spray out and scald. If it overheats again after topping up, have it towed rather than driving on.

## The rest of the cooling system

Coolant is only one part of the system. A few other parts wear out with age and are worth knowing:

- **Water pump.** It circulates the coolant. Signs of trouble include leaks near the front of the engine, a whining noise, or overheating. On many engines it's driven by the timing belt and replaced along with it.
- **Thermostat.** It controls engine temperature. A stuck thermostat can cause overheating, or an engine that takes a long time to warm up and a weak heater.
- **Radiator and hoses.** Hoses can crack, soften or swell with age. Squeeze them gently when the engine is cold: they should feel firm, not brittle or mushy.
- **Radiator cap.** It holds the system's pressure, and a worn seal can let coolant boil off.

## What a coolant change involves

A coolant service drains the old coolant, sometimes flushes the system, and refills it with the right type and mix. Air pockets must be bled out, a step that varies by car. Many owners leave it to a shop, and coolant must be disposed of properly. It's toxic to people and animals and can taste sweet, so wipe up spills and keep it away from pets and children.

Record the date, the mileage and the coolant used.
