<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> it depends on the car, because many modern gasoline cars have the fuel filter built into the fuel pump assembly inside the tank, with no scheduled replacement. Older cars often have a separate filter in the fuel line that's replaced on a mileage interval, and diesel engines often have shorter filter intervals. Check the maintenance schedule in your owner's manual: if it lists a fuel filter, follow that interval.</p>

A fuel filter is something some people pay for unnecessarily, and something some owners of older cars and diesels forget entirely. The difference comes down to which kind your car has.

[[cta]]

## What the fuel filter does

The fuel filter catches dirt, rust and debris in the fuel before it reaches the injectors, which have very fine openings. A clogged filter restricts fuel flow, which can starve the engine under load.

## Lifetime in-tank filters

On many modern gasoline cars, the filter is part of the fuel pump module inside the tank. Manufacturers often don't list a replacement interval for it, because it's intended to last a long time, often as long as the pump. If it does clog, it's usually replaced together with the pump.

If your manual's maintenance schedule doesn't list a fuel filter, that's usually why. A shop that recommends a fuel filter replacement on such a car should be able to show you where the filter is.

## Serviceable filters

Some cars have a separate filter in the fuel line, often under the car or in the engine bay. Older owner's manuals commonly listed replacing it at a set mileage. If your car has one, the interval is in the manual. Gasoline fuel lines can hold pressure and fuel is flammable, so most owners leave filter changes to a shop.

## Diesel fuel filters

Diesel engines are especially sensitive to dirt and water in the fuel, and their high-pressure injection systems are expensive to repair. So diesel fuel filters:

- usually have a regular replacement interval,
- often include a **water separator** that collects water and needs draining at intervals,
- may have a warning light that comes on when water needs draining.

Follow the diesel-specific schedule in your manual closely. If you buy fuel somewhere you're unsure of, or the water warning comes on, deal with it promptly.

## Diesel: draining the water separator

On many diesel vehicles the fuel filter includes a water separator, and the owner's manual explains how to drain it. In general:

- **Watch for the water-in-fuel warning light.** When it comes on, drain the separator as soon as you safely can, following the manual's steps.
- **Drain it at the interval listed in the manual**, even if no light comes on.
- **Be careful with the drained fuel.** Catch it in a container and dispose of it properly.
- **Fuel is flammable.** Work with the engine off and cool, away from flames and sparks, and follow the manual's steps. If you're unsure, have a shop do it.
- **Some systems need priming** after a filter change to remove air from the fuel lines. The manual describes the procedure; some vehicles do it automatically.

If the warning comes back soon after draining, a lot of water may have gotten into the tank, which needs a shop's attention.

## Signs of a clogged fuel filter

- **Hesitation or loss of power** when accelerating, climbing hills or towing.
- **Hard starting** or long cranking.
- **Rough idle or misfires.**
- **Stalling**, especially under load.
- **A check engine light** with a fuel pressure or lean-mixture code.

These overlap with other faults, especially a weak fuel pump, a pressure regulator problem or dirty injectors. Have the cause diagnosed rather than replacing parts on a guess.

## Old fuel and stored cars

Gasoline breaks down over months, and old fuel can leave deposits that clog filters and injectors. If a car sits for long periods, keeping the tank reasonably full and using a fuel stabilizer helps; see [low-mileage car maintenance](/guides/low-mileage-car-maintenance/).

## The fuel pump and the filter

On cars with an in-tank filter, the fuel pump and filter are usually one assembly, so problems with either are diagnosed together. A few things worth knowing:

- **A whining noise from the rear of the car**, especially when the tank is low, can come from a struggling fuel pump.
- **Fuel helps cool the pump** on many in-tank designs. Some mechanics recommend not regularly driving on a nearly empty tank for that reason; your owner's manual may give its own advice.
- **Fuel pressure tests** let a shop tell a restricted filter from a weak pump, so ask for a diagnosis before paying for parts.

## Keeping track

If your car has a serviceable filter, record the date and mileage each time it's changed, since it's a part that's out of sight and easy to forget. On a diesel, record the water drains too.
