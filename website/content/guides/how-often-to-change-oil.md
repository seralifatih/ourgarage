<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> most cars on the road today need an oil change every 5,000 to 10,000 miles, or every 6 to 12 months, whichever comes first. The exact number for your car is in the maintenance section of your owner's manual. If you drive mostly short trips, tow, or live somewhere very hot, cold or dusty, your manual probably puts you on a shorter "severe" schedule.</p>

The old rule of an oil change every 3,000 miles comes from engines and oils that are decades behind what's in most driveways now. Following it won't hurt your engine, but for most cars it means paying for oil changes you don't need.

[[cta]]

## Where your real interval comes from

Your car's manufacturer tested the engine with a specific oil and set an interval based on that testing. That interval is printed in the maintenance schedule of the owner's manual, usually as two numbers: a mileage and a time limit. If you don't have the paper manual, most manufacturers publish owner's manuals on their websites, and many put the schedule in the car's infotainment system or app.

A few things to look for when you read it:

- **Two schedules.** Many manuals list a normal schedule and a severe (or "special operating conditions") schedule with shorter intervals. More on that below.
- **The oil spec.** The manual names a viscosity, such as 0W-20 or 5W-30, and often an industry or manufacturer approval. The interval assumes you use oil that meets it.
- **"Whichever comes first."** The mileage and the time limit both count. Reaching either one means it's due.

## Normal vs. severe driving

The word "severe" makes it sound like a schedule for off-roaders and race cars. It isn't. Manuals typically put you on the severe schedule if a lot of your driving looks like this:

- Short trips where the engine doesn't fully warm up, especially in cold weather
- Lots of stop-and-go traffic or long periods of idling
- Towing a trailer or carrying heavy loads
- Dusty, sandy or gravel roads
- Very hot or very cold climates

Plenty of ordinary commutes fit that list. If yours does, use the shorter interval. Short trips are the one people miss most often: an engine that never gets fully hot can't burn off the moisture and fuel that collect in the oil, so the oil wears out faster than the odometer suggests.

## Why the time limit matters

Oil breaks down with age, not just with miles. Moisture and combustion byproducts build up in it even when the car mostly sits. That's why a car driven 3,000 miles a year still needs an oil change, usually once or twice a year depending on what the manual says.

This is the limit people forget. If you drive little, nothing about the car reminds you; the odometer never gets near the number on the sticker. A date-based reminder is the simplest fix.

## Oil life monitors

Many newer cars have an oil life monitor that shows a percentage or a "service due" message. It doesn't test the oil itself. It estimates oil life from how the engine has been used: engine revolutions, temperatures, trip lengths and similar data. In practice that means it already accounts for some of the severe-driving conditions above.

If your manual says to change the oil when the monitor tells you to, follow the monitor. Two caveats:

1. Most manuals still give a maximum time between changes, even if the monitor shows oil life left.
2. The monitor needs to be reset after each change. If a shop forgets, the next reading will be wrong.

## Should you check the oil between changes?

Yes. An oil change interval assumes the engine has the right amount of oil the whole time. Some engines use a little oil between changes, and a leak can drop the level without any warning light until it's low enough to cause damage.

Check the dipstick about once a month, or whatever your manual recommends, with the car parked on level ground. Some cars have no dipstick and show the level on the dashboard instead; the manual explains how to read it. If you find yourself adding oil regularly, mention it to a mechanic.

## How often to change synthetic oil

Use the oil type your manual specifies. Many newer engines require full synthetic oil, and the longer intervals in their manuals assume it. Synthetic oil resists breaking down better than conventional oil, which is why engines that use it often have intervals somewhere around 7,500 to 10,000 miles or more. But the interval belongs to the engine, not the oil: the number in your manual is the one to follow.

- **If your manual calls for synthetic**, follow its interval, and its severe schedule if your driving fits it.
- **If your manual allows conventional oil**, it may list a different interval for it. Switching to synthetic is fine, but don't stretch the interval beyond what the manual says just because the bottle advertises a longer life.
- **Synthetic blends** sit between the two. Treat them as the manual says for the oil spec you're using.

Whatever the oil, the time limit in the manual still applies.

## The oil filter

The oil filter traps the particles the oil collects as it circulates. Most owner's manuals call for a new filter at every oil change, and that's what most shops do: a clogged filter can't clean the new oil, and filters are cheap compared with the oil itself. A few older manuals allowed changing the filter at every other oil change; if yours says so, that's fine to follow, but replacing it every time is the simple, safe habit.

Use a filter that meets the manufacturer's specification for your engine. Some newer engines use a cartridge filter that sits in a housing rather than a spin-on canister; the procedure is a little different but the interval is the same.

## How to know when your next change is due

You need three pieces of information: the date and mileage of your last oil change, and your interval. From those you can work out the next due date and the next due mileage. Whichever arrives first is your deadline.

The weak link is usually remembering the first two. A windshield sticker falls off or shows the shop's generic interval. The receipt is in the glovebox, or nowhere. And if more than one person drives the car, nobody's sure who handled it last.

A simple system:

1. **Log it when it happens.** Write down the date, the mileage, what was done (oil and filter, and the oil type) and where. Do it at the shop or in your driveway, before you forget.
2. **Set a reminder for both limits.** For example, every 7,500 miles or 12 months, using your manual's numbers.
3. **Keep the odometer current.** A mileage reminder only works if it knows roughly how far you've driven, so update the mileage every few weeks.
4. **Keep the receipts.** They prove the work was done on schedule, which matters for warranty claims and when you sell the car. See our guide to [keeping service records for resale](/guides/car-service-records-for-resale/).

Oil changes are also a good anchor for other jobs. Many people get their [tires rotated](/guides/how-often-to-rotate-tires/) at every oil change or every other one, and shops often check the air filter at the same visit. For the full picture of what's due when, see the [car maintenance schedule by mileage](/guides/car-maintenance-schedule-by-mileage/).

## What happens if you go overdue?

Going a few hundred miles over once won't ruin a healthy engine. The risk builds when it becomes a habit. Old oil protects less, and over time sludge and deposits can form, which raise wear on parts that are expensive to fix. Overdue oil changes are also exactly the kind of gap a used-car buyer or a warranty claim will look for.

If you've gone well past the interval, change the oil soon, check the level now, and put a reminder in place so it doesn't happen again.
