<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> most cars need an oil change every 5,000 to 10,000 miles, a tire rotation every 5,000 to 7,500 miles, air filters every 15,000 to 30,000 miles, and fluid and part replacements at larger milestones such as 30,000, 60,000 and 100,000 miles. Every car's schedule is different, so the table below is a starting point. Copy the exact numbers from your owner's manual.</p>

A maintenance schedule only works if you know three things: what's due, when it's due, and when it was last done. This guide covers the first two with general ranges. The third is up to you, and it's where most people lose track.

[[cta]]

## Common intervals at a glance

These are general ranges you'll see across many owner's manuals. Your car's numbers may sit anywhere in the range or outside it. Use them to know what to look for in your manual, not as a substitute for it.

| Item | Common interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Engine oil and filter | 5,000–10,000 miles or 6–12 months | Shorter on the severe schedule. See [how often to change oil](/guides/how-often-to-change-oil/). |
| Tire rotation | 5,000–7,500 miles | Often done with the oil change. See [how often to rotate tires](/guides/how-often-to-rotate-tires/). |
| Tire pressure | Monthly | Plus before long trips. |
| Wiper blades | 6–12 months | Or when they streak or chatter. |
| Engine air filter | 15,000–30,000 miles | Sooner on dusty roads. Often inspected at oil changes. |
| Cabin air filter | 15,000–30,000 miles or yearly | Affects A/C airflow and smells. |
| Brake fluid | Every 2–3 years in many manuals | Some manuals list inspection only. |
| Coolant | About 30,000 miles for older types; 100,000+ miles for long-life coolant | Time limits apply too. |
| Spark plugs | About 30,000 miles (copper) to 100,000+ miles (iridium/platinum) | Most newer cars use long-life plugs. |
| Transmission fluid | Roughly 30,000–100,000 miles | Some manuals say "lifetime" under normal use but list a change on the severe schedule. |
| Timing belt (if equipped) | Commonly 60,000–105,000 miles | Many engines use a timing chain instead, with no set interval. |
| Battery | Often lasts 3–5 years | Heat shortens it. Have it tested once it's a few years old. |
| Brake pads and rotors | No fixed interval | Inspect at rotations; replace on wear. |

## Reading your owner's manual schedule

Manuals present schedules in a few ways. Some are tables by mileage (every 5,000 miles, every 15,000, every 30,000). Some list each item with its own interval. Many cars now show the schedule or "maintenance minder" codes on the dashboard instead of printing a full table.

Three things to check when you read yours:

- **Normal vs. severe.** Most manuals include a shorter schedule for severe conditions: short trips, stop-and-go traffic, towing, dust, and extreme heat or cold. A lot of everyday driving qualifies. Decide which schedule fits you and stick to it.
- **"Inspect" vs. "replace."** Inspect means look at it and replace if needed. Replace means change it regardless of how it looks. Shops sometimes blur these.
- **Whichever comes first.** Almost every interval has a mileage and a time limit. Fluids and rubber parts age even if the car doesn't move.

If you've lost the manual, check the manufacturer's owner website. Most publish manuals and maintenance schedules by model and year.

## Milestone by milestone

Here's how a typical schedule groups together. Again, your car's exact contents for each milestone come from the manual.

### Every oil change

Oil and filter, a tire rotation if your interval lines up, a check of all fluid levels, a look at the tires, lights and wiper blades, and on many cars an engine air filter inspection. This is the visit that keeps everything else on track, because someone looks at the car regularly.

### Every 15,000 to 30,000 miles

Air filters (engine and cabin) are the usual items here. Many schedules also call for a more thorough inspection of brakes, suspension, exhaust and hoses at this point.

### Around 30,000 miles

Older or simpler engines may be due for spark plugs. Some manuals list transmission fluid or coolant here, especially on the severe schedule. Brake fluid often comes up by time before mileage.

### Around 60,000 miles

Common items include spark plugs on some engines, transmission fluid on many schedules, and the timing belt on some engines with a belt. Brakes and tires frequently need replacing around this range, though that depends on wear, not a fixed number.

### Around 100,000 miles

Long-life spark plugs, long-life coolant, and timing belts on many engines that have one. Hoses, drive belts and suspension parts get a closer look. A car that reaches 100,000 miles with a complete service history is a much easier sell than one without; see our guide to [keeping service records for resale](/guides/car-service-records-for-resale/).

## Dealer service packages

Dealers often sell "30k", "60k" and "90k" services. They're based on the manufacturer's schedule, which is useful, but the package price sometimes includes extras the manual doesn't call for at that mileage, such as fuel system cleanings or fluid flushes ahead of schedule. Before you approve one, put the package list next to your manual's schedule and ask about anything that doesn't match. You're under no obligation to buy items that aren't in the manual.

## Turning the schedule into reminders

A schedule printed in the manual doesn't remind you of anything. The practical setup is a short list of recurring reminders, each with the interval from your manual:

1. **Oil change:** mileage and time, whichever comes first
2. **Tire rotation:** mileage, or tied to every oil change
3. **Air filters:** mileage, or check at every oil change
4. **Brake fluid:** time (if your manual gives one)
5. **Wiper blades:** time
6. **Big-milestone items:** spark plugs, coolant, transmission fluid, timing belt, each at its mileage

Then log each service when it's done, with the date and mileage, so the next due point is calculated from what actually happened rather than from when you meant to do it. Update the odometer reading every few weeks so mileage reminders know where you are.

If you drive very little, lean on the time limits. If you drive a lot, the mileage limits will come first, sometimes more often than you'd guess.
