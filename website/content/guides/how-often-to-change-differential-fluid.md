<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> differential fluid intervals vary more than most, from around 30,000 to 60,000 miles in many owner's manuals to much longer, or inspection only, in others. Towing, heavy loads and off-road use usually call for shorter intervals. Your owner's manual lists the interval for each differential your vehicle has.</p>

The differential is one of the parts most owners never think about, partly because many cars don't have a separately serviced one. If yours does, its fluid is easy to miss.

[[cta]]

## What a differential does

When a car turns, the outside wheels travel farther than the inside ones. The differential lets the driven wheels turn at different speeds while still delivering power to both. It's a set of gears running in its own oil, usually a thick gear oil.

## Does your car have one to service?

Every car has a differential, but how it's serviced depends on the drivetrain:

- **Front-wheel drive:** the differential is usually built into the transaxle and often shares fluid with the transmission. In that case there's no separate differential service; see [how often to change transmission fluid](/guides/how-often-to-change-transmission-fluid/). Some front-wheel-drive transmissions do have a separate differential fluid, so check the manual.
- **Rear-wheel drive:** a separate rear differential with its own gear oil.
- **All-wheel drive and four-wheel drive:** often a front differential, a rear differential and a transfer case or power transfer unit, each with its own fluid and interval.

The maintenance schedule in your owner's manual lists each one.

## How often to change it

Intervals vary by maker and model. Common patterns:

- **A fixed mileage**, often somewhere around 30,000 to 60,000 miles.
- **Longer intervals or "inspect only"** for some vehicles under normal use.
- **Shorter intervals for severe use**: towing, heavy loads, off-roading, driving through deep water.

Driving through water deep enough to reach the axle can let water into a differential through its vent. Many manuals call for checking or changing the fluid after that. Milky or foamy fluid is a sign water got in.

If your driving fits the severe schedule for engine oil, it may well fit it for the differential too. See [how often to change your oil](/guides/how-often-to-change-oil/) for what counts as severe.

## Limited-slip differentials

Some vehicles have a limited-slip differential, which needs a specific fluid or a friction-modifier additive. Using the wrong fluid can cause chatter or noise in turns. If your vehicle has one, the manual will say which fluid to use; mention it to the shop doing the service.

## Checking the fluid level

Most differentials have no dipstick. On many, the level is checked by removing the fill plug on the side of the housing: with the vehicle level, the fluid should come up to around the bottom of the fill hole. The exact method varies, and some designs differ, so follow your manual or have a shop check it. It means working under the vehicle, which needs a lift or proper jack stands, never just a jack. If you're changing the fluid, make sure you can open the fill plug before removing the drain plug, so you aren't left with a differential you can't refill.

A useful habit is to ask for the differential level to be checked whenever the vehicle is on a lift for an oil change or tire rotation, especially if you tow. It takes a few minutes, and low fluid caught early saves a much bigger repair.

## Signs of a differential problem

- **A whine or howl** that rises and falls with road speed.
- **Clunking** when setting off, or when shifting between drive and reverse.
- **Rumbling or grinding in turns.**
- **Gear oil leaks**: a thick, often strong-smelling fluid under the axle.
- **Vibration** that gets worse with speed.

Low fluid from a leaking seal can be a cause, and it is much cheaper to fix early than worn gears. These symptoms also overlap with worn tires, wheel bearings, universal joints and the driveshaft, so get a proper diagnosis.

## Transfer cases and AWD units

On four-wheel-drive and many all-wheel-drive vehicles, the transfer case or power transfer unit splits power between the front and rear axles. It has its own fluid, and on some vehicles it's the item most often overlooked:

- **The fluid type varies**: some use gear oil, some automatic transmission fluid, some a specific manufacturer fluid.
- **The interval varies too**, and towing or off-road use usually shortens it.
- **Some AWD couplings** have their own fluid and service interval as well.

Your owner's manual lists every unit that needs servicing. If it lists more than one, keep separate records for each.

## What the service involves

Changing differential fluid usually means removing a drain plug or the differential cover, draining the old oil, and refilling through a fill plug to the correct level. It's quicker and cheaper than many services. A good shop checks the drained oil and the magnetic drain plug. A fine metallic paste is common, but chunks or flakes can point to wear.

Record each fluid separately if your vehicle has more than one: front differential, rear differential and transfer case often have different intervals. For where these fit among everything else, see the [car maintenance schedule by mileage](/guides/car-maintenance-schedule-by-mileage/).
