<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> many owner's manuals call for new brake fluid every two to three years, regardless of how far you drive. Brake fluid ages with time, not miles, because it absorbs moisture from the air. Some manuals give a mileage too, and some only list an inspection, so check yours for the exact interval.</p>

Brake fluid is the item people are most surprised to find on their maintenance schedule. The car stops fine and the reservoir looks full, so it seems like there's nothing to do. The reason there is comes down to water.

[[cta]]

## Why brake fluid ages

When you press the brake pedal, brake fluid carries that force to the brakes at each wheel. Most brake fluids are glycol-based and hygroscopic, which means they slowly absorb moisture from the air through hoses and seals over time.

Water in the fluid causes two problems:

- **It lowers the boiling point.** Hard or repeated braking, such as on a long downhill, heats the fluid. If it boils, vapor bubbles form, and the pedal can go soft just when you need it most.
- **It promotes corrosion** inside brake lines, calipers and the ABS unit, which are costly to replace.

None of this shows on the reservoir. The fluid can look clean and full while its boiling point has dropped, which is why the change is scheduled by time.

## How often to change it

The interval is in the maintenance schedule of your owner's manual. Common patterns:

- **Every two to three years**, regardless of mileage. This is the most common.
- **A time and a mileage**, whichever comes first.
- **Inspect only.** Some manuals list a regular inspection rather than a fixed change. A shop can test the fluid and advise.

Because the interval is time-based, it matters just as much for cars that are rarely driven. See [low-mileage car maintenance](/guides/low-mileage-car-maintenance/) for the other items that age by the calendar.

## Types of brake fluid

The type is printed on the reservoir cap and in the manual, usually as a DOT rating:

- **DOT 3, DOT 4 and DOT 5.1** are glycol-based. DOT 4 and DOT 5.1 have higher boiling points than DOT 3. Despite its number, DOT 5.1 is glycol-based, not silicone.
- **DOT 5** is silicone-based and is not interchangeable with the others.

Use only the type your manual specifies, and never mix silicone DOT 5 with the glycol types.

## How to check it

- **Level.** The reservoir, often on the driver's side near the back of the engine bay (the manual shows where), has minimum and maximum marks. Check with the car parked on level ground.
- **Condition.** Fresh fluid is usually clear to light amber. Dark fluid is old, though color alone isn't a reliable test.
- **Moisture.** Shops can test the fluid's moisture content or boiling point with a tester or test strips. This is the best way to know whether it needs changing.

## When the level drops

As brake pads wear, the pistons in the calipers move out and more fluid sits in the calipers, so the reservoir level falls slowly. That's normal; it usually rises again when new pads are fitted. See [how long brake pads last](/guides/how-long-do-brake-pads-last/) for the signs pads are due.

A level that drops quickly, or below the minimum mark, can mean a leak. Have the brakes checked rather than just topping up.

## Warning signs

- A **soft or spongy pedal**, or one that sinks toward the floor.
- The **brake warning light**, which on many cars covers low fluid.
- **Fluid on the inside of a wheel** or under the car.

Any of these needs a prompt check. Brake problems aren't something to wait on.

## Brake fluid after other brake work

Some brake jobs disturb the fluid or let air into the system, and the shop should bleed the brakes afterwards:

- **Replacing a caliper, brake hose or line** opens the hydraulic system.
- **Replacing the master cylinder** or ABS unit.
- **Any work where the reservoir ran dry.**

A routine pad change usually doesn't require new fluid, but it's a good moment to check its condition and ask when it was last changed. If the answer is "never" or "years ago", doing it at the same visit saves a separate trip.

## What the service involves

Changing brake fluid usually means bleeding the system: pushing new fluid through each brake line until the old fluid is out, and making sure no air is left in the lines. Cars with ABS sometimes need a specific procedure or a scan tool. Many owners leave it to a shop; it is usually a short visit.

Write down the date. A two- or three-year interval is easy to lose, and a time-based reminder is the simplest way not to. For how it fits with the rest of the schedule, see the [car maintenance schedule by mileage](/guides/car-maintenance-schedule-by-mileage/).
