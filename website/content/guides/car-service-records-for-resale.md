<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> for every service, record the date, the odometer reading, what was done (including parts and fluids), who did it, and what it cost, and keep the receipt or invoice. Store the receipts in one folder and keep a running log in date order. When you sell, hand the buyer a one-page summary plus the receipts. A complete, consistent history is one of the clearest signs that a used car was looked after.</p>

Most people do the maintenance and then lose the evidence. The receipts scatter across the glovebox, email and a kitchen drawer, and by the time you sell the car you can't prove the timing belt was replaced. Keeping records takes about a minute per service if you do it at the time.

[[cta]]

## Why records matter when you sell

A used car buyer is trying to figure out how the car was treated. They can't see inside the engine, so they look for evidence. A clear service history answers their main questions:

- Were oil changes done regularly, or with long gaps?
- Were the big-ticket milestone items done, such as a timing belt, spark plugs or transmission fluid, if the schedule called for them?
- When were the tires and brakes last replaced?
- Were recalls taken care of?

A buyer who can see that has fewer unknowns to worry about, which makes the car easier to sell and gives you something concrete to point to when they negotiate. A dealer appraising a trade-in looks for the same things.

Records matter while you own the car too. If you need to make a warranty claim, the manufacturer may ask you to show the required maintenance was done. The FTC has said a manufacturer can't void your warranty just because you used an independent shop or did the work yourself, but you still need to be able to show the work was done.

## What to record for every service

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Date | March 14, 2026 |
| Odometer | 48,210 miles |
| Work done | Oil and filter change, tire rotation |
| Parts and fluids | 5 qt 0W-20 full synthetic, OEM filter |
| Who did it | Main Street Auto, or "DIY" |
| Cost | $89.40 |
| Receipt | Invoice #, or a photo of it |

The odometer reading is the field people skip most often, and it's one of the most important. Most of the maintenance schedule runs on mileage, so a date alone doesn't show whether a service was on time.

Also note things that weren't scheduled maintenance: repairs, warranty work, recall work, new tires, a new battery, windshield replacement. And note anything a mechanic recommended that you haven't done yet, so it doesn't get lost.

## Organizing receipts

Pick one system and use it every time.

- **Paper:** an accordion folder or a binder with sleeves, in date order. Keep it in the house, not in the car, so it doesn't get lost if the car is stolen or totaled.
- **Digital:** photograph each receipt as soon as you get it and save it with the date and mileage in the file name, or attach it to the entry in a maintenance log. Shops that email invoices make this easier; save them to one place.
- **Both:** a digital log for quick reference and searching, and the paper originals for the buyer. This is the most robust option.

Thermal-paper receipts fade within a few years. Photograph them early.

## Do-it-yourself maintenance

DIY work counts, but it needs more supporting detail because there's no shop invoice:

- Keep the receipt for the oil, filter and parts. It shows the brand and spec.
- Write down the date and odometer reading in your log the same day.
- Take a photo of the odometer, ideally with the new part or the oil bottle in the frame.

This also covers you for warranty purposes.

## Vehicle history reports have gaps

Buyers often check a vehicle history report, such as Carfax or AutoCheck. These reports are useful, but they only show records that shops and other sources report to them. DIY work won't appear, and neither will work from many independent shops. A car with perfect maintenance can have a thin report.

Your own records fill those gaps. When a buyer asks why the report shows only two oil changes in five years, you can hand them the rest.

## Check for open recalls

Before you sell, look up the VIN on NHTSA's recall site (nhtsa.gov/recalls). Recall repairs are free at a dealer. Having open recalls fixed, and keeping the paperwork, removes a question the buyer would otherwise ask.

## Presenting the history when you sell

Make it easy for the buyer to see the pattern:

1. **A one-page summary** in date order: date, mileage, service. A buyer can scan it in a minute.
2. **The receipts behind it,** in the same order.
3. **Milestone items highlighted:** timing belt, spark plugs, fluids, tires, brakes, battery.
4. **The owner's manual and both keys,** if you have them. Buyers notice.

If you've kept a digital log, export it or print it for the summary.

## Keeping it going with more than one driver

The history only stays complete if every service gets logged, no matter who took the car in. In a household where several people handle the car, records go missing because each person assumes the receipt went somewhere. Agree on one shared log and log each service at the shop, when the details are in front of you. For what should be in that history at each mileage, see the [car maintenance schedule by mileage](/guides/car-maintenance-schedule-by-mileage/).
