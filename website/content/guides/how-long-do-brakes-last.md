<p class="answer"><strong>Short answer:</strong> "the brakes" are several parts that wear at very different rates: pads wear fastest, while rotors usually last through one or two sets of pads and are replaced once they wear below a minimum thickness. Calipers, hoses and lines can last much of the car's life, and brake fluid ages on a time schedule. Your owner's manual gives inspection intervals, and worn parts are replaced when inspection shows they're due.</p>

Pads are the part people usually mean, and we cover them in detail in [how long do brake pads last](/guides/how-long-do-brake-pads-last/). This guide is about everything else, especially the rotors.

[[cta]]

## The parts of a disc brake

Most cars today have disc brakes at least on the front wheels:

- **Rotors (discs):** metal discs that turn with the wheel.
- **Pads:** friction material that clamps onto the rotors.
- **Calipers:** hold the pads and push them against the rotor when you brake.
- **Brake lines and hoses:** carry fluid from the pedal to each caliper.
- **Brake fluid:** transmits the pedal force. It ages with time; see [how often to change brake fluid](/guides/how-often-to-change-brake-fluid/).

Some cars have drum brakes on the rear wheels, which use shoes inside a drum and generally wear more slowly.

## How long rotors last

Every time the pads clamp the rotor, a small amount of rotor wears away too, just much more slowly than the pads. Many rotors last through one or two sets of pads, but there's no reliable mileage. It depends on:

- **How you drive**, especially hard braking and city traffic.
- **Rotor design and thickness.** Many modern rotors are fairly thin from new.
- **Heat.** Towing, mountain driving and repeated hard stops create heat that can wear or distort rotors.
- **Corrosion.** Cars that sit, or are driven on salted winter roads, can get rust on the rotor faces.
- **Pad condition.** Pads worn down to the backing plate can score a rotor badly in a short distance.

Each rotor has a minimum thickness set by the maker, and on many rotors it's stamped on the edge or hub, often marked "MIN TH" or similar. Once a rotor wears below it, it must be replaced.

## Signs your rotors need attention

- **Pulsing or vibration** through the pedal or steering wheel when braking.
- **Deep grooves or scoring** on the rotor face, visible through the wheel.
- **A lip** around the outer edge where the pad doesn't reach.
- **Blue or dark heat marks.**
- **Grinding**, which can mean pads are worn through and metal is cutting into the rotor. Avoid driving until it has been inspected.

## Resurface or replace?

When pads are replaced, a shop measures the rotors. If they're above minimum thickness and in good shape, they may be reused or resurfaced, which cuts a thin layer off to restore a flat surface. Resurfacing removes material, so the rotor must still be above its minimum afterwards. Many modern rotors are thin enough that replacing them is the more common choice.

Rotors, like pads, are replaced in axle pairs so the car brakes evenly.

## Calipers, hoses and lines

These usually last much longer, but they can fail:

- **Sticking calipers** can make the car pull to one side, wear one pad much faster than the other, or make a wheel hot after driving. More common on cars that sit a lot or face road salt.
- **Rubber hoses** can crack or bulge with age.
- **Metal lines** can corrode, especially in regions that salt the roads.

A good brake inspection covers all of these, not just the pads.

**Don't keep driving if** the pedal feels soft or sinks toward the floor, you see brake fluid leaking, a red brake light stays on with the parking brake released, or the car pulls hard to one side when braking. Have it inspected or towed.

## The parking brake

The parking brake wears and needs attention too, though it's often forgotten:

- **Cable-operated parking brakes** can stretch over time, so the lever or pedal travels farther before it holds. Many can be adjusted.
- **Rear drum or "drum-in-hat" parking brakes** have their own shoes that wear slowly.
- **Electric parking brakes** often need a service mode, set with a scan tool or a button sequence in the manual, before the rear pads can be replaced. Forcing the caliper piston back without it can damage the brake.

Use the parking brake regularly. One that's rarely applied can seize, especially on cars that sit or face road salt.

## When to have the brakes inspected

Follow the inspection interval in your manual. A practical habit is to have the brakes checked at every [tire rotation](/guides/how-often-to-rotate-tires/), when the wheels are already off. Ask for the pad thickness and rotor condition to be noted on the invoice, and keep it. Over two or three services you'll see how fast your brakes wear and know when the next job is coming.
